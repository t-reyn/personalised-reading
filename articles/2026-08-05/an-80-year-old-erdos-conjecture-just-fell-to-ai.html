An 80-Year-Old Erdős Conjecture Just Fell to AI — and a Backlog Nobody Can Read Is Piling Up Behind It

5 August 2026 · synthesised from 1 source

OpenAI's unreleased model produced a proof of an 80-year-old Erdős conjecture strong enough that a Fields medallist says it would pass peer review at the world's top maths journal; most of the hundreds of other AI-generated "solutions" now flooding the field's central problem tracker haven't been checked by anyone qualified to catch a mistake.

On 20 May 2026, an unreleased OpenAI model handed mathematicians something they didn't expect: a counterexample to a conjecture Paul Erdős posed almost 80 years ago, built using algebraic number theory techniques nobody had made work for it before. Tim Gowers, a Fields medallist and one of the field's most demanding reviewers, said the proof would clear peer review at the Annals of Mathematics, the discipline's top journal, adding that "no previous AI-generated proof has come close to that." Jacob Tsimerman, another Fields medallist, called it "a really impressive piece of work... definitely an intimidating construction," then left academia for OpenAI two months later. It's the clearest evidence yet that AI can do original mathematical research, not just walk students through problems it has already seen solved.

This wasn't a lucky one-off

The result sits inside a bigger pattern. Thomas Bloom, a mathematician who has spent since 2023 cataloguing every problem Erdős ever posed at erdosproblems.com, now lists 1,217 entries, 565 marked solved. Since Bloom added a comments feature in August 2025, several unrelated systems have chalked up wins on the tracker. In January, two independent researchers used GPT-5.2 Pro to close problem 728, on the divisibility of factorials. That same month a 24-person Google DeepMind team combed the backlog, solved four problems outright, and turned up nine more that had quietly already been solved without anyone noticing. In May, a second DeepMind team ran 353 of the site's problems through a formal proof system and autonomously resolved nine of them for a few hundred dollars each. OpenAI's newer "Astra" system added three further solved problems to the tally on 1 August alone. Noga Alon, a Princeton mathematician who had worked several of these problems by hand, put down his own attempts once the machines caught up: "once AI started to solve them, there is no point anymore."

Why "formalised" is the word doing the real work

That 353-problem figure from DeepMind's second run matters more than the raw solve count, because those particular problems weren't just answered in English. They were autoformalised: translated out of plain language into Lean, the formal system mathematicians use to write machine-checkable proofs, so software can grade every logical step the way a compiler checks code rather than a person skimming it and taking the model's word for it. When a result comes out of that pipeline, verifying it is close to free. Most results landing on the site right now don't come out of that pipeline at all.

The bottleneck is the same one showing up everywhere else

Bloom has flagged the real risk bluntly: much of what lands in his comments section is plain-English chatbot output from people without the training to check it, producing what he called "100- to 200-page papers... no human has read." One of the researchers most active on the site, Kevin Barreto, publicly admitted to claiming an AI had solved a problem that, on closer look, Erdős himself had already published a solution to back in 1977; the model hadn't found anything new, it just hadn't been told the problem was closed. His own conclusion was blunt: researchers need to "put greater focus on literature search."

Wouter van Doorn, another regular contributor, made the more optimistic version of the same point: the proofs he ends up publishing after cleaning up an AI's draft are "simpler, more general, and easier to read... than the thing that ChatGPT came up with," the model gets there first, but a person still has to make the result usable, not just correct. It's the same limiting factor flagged here two days ago in software development: once a system can produce output, whether code or a proof, faster than anyone can read it, the ceiling on what's safe to trust stops being model quality and becomes how cheaply the output can be checked. Mathematics has just given that pattern its most concrete demonstration yet.

The number to watch

Astra's three fresh solutions two days before this reporting landed show the run rate is still climbing, not levelling off. The figure worth tracking over the next few months isn't how many Erdős problems fall next. It's what share of new AI-attributed solutions arrive pre-formalised and machine-verified, against how many are just more plain-English pages joining a queue nobody has the hours to check.
