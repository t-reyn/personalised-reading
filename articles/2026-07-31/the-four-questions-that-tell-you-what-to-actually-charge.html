The Four Questions That Tell You What to Actually Charge

31 July 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

Anchoring, a value metric, and a four-question survey you can run on your waitlist this week — three concrete moves that replace the guess most solo builders make when they type a number into a pricing page.

Patrick McKenzie, who has priced software professionally for two decades, gives founders a specific instruction when they show him a first pricing page: take the number and multiply it by ten to fifty. Not because customers won't notice, but because most solo builders anchor their price against other apps instead of against what the alternative costs the buyer, and that single choice caps the number before the market ever gets a say. You've already worked out your monetisation model, subscription over one-off, maybe a free tier to pull people in. What's missing is the number itself, and there are three concrete ways to stop guessing it.

Anchor against the real alternative, not the app store

A price only looks expensive or cheap relative to a reference point, and if the only reference point in the room is "other apps like mine", you've quietly capped yourself at app-store pricing before you've typed a digit. McKenzie's own consulting cases make the point bluntly: he told the founder of a crypto tax tool to price against the accountant an affected user would otherwise pay roughly $5,000 to hire, and told an ecommerce optimisation tool to price against a conversion-rate agency charging a minimum $5,000 a month, or a specialist hire running $100,000 a year. Against those anchors, a $250-a-month tool is a rounding error. Against "other apps", it's outrageous.

The same move works at consumer scale. A workout-and-nutrition logging app sitting at $7.99 a month looks steep next to a free calorie counter. Anchored against a single personal-training session (commonly $90–$120 in an Australian capital) or one dietitian consult (around $150), that same product could sit at $19–$24 a month and still read as trivial against the thing it replaces.

Pick a value metric that scales with what you deliver

The axis you charge on is a message about what you think the customer's value is, not just an admin choice. CartHook charges a flat $300 a month regardless of usage, which suits a single well-defined feature set but leaves money on the table with heavy users. Slack does the opposite: it bills only for seats that are active, which removes the biggest objection to inviting a large team and is a large part of why the model spread so fast. Across SaaS generally, pricing pages settle around 3.5 tiers on average, enough to separate a light user from a heavy one without turning the page into a spreadsheet.

For a solo builder the test is simple: does your price move with the value the customer is extracting, or is it flat no matter how hard they use the product? A booking tool for personal trainers charging a flat $15 a month treats a coach with four clients the same as one with forty. Pricing per active client instead ties the number to the coach's own growth, and a growing customer paying more doesn't feel like a penalty, it feels proportionate.

Ask before you build the paywall

Anchoring and a value metric still leave a range, not a number, and the cheapest way to narrow it is the price sensitivity meter Dutch economist Peter van Westendorp published in 1976: ask respondents four questions, at what price is this so cheap you'd doubt the quality, so cheap it's a bargain, starting to get expensive but you'd still consider it, and so expensive you wouldn't buy it at all. Plot the cumulative answers and two crossing points fall out: an acceptable range, bounded by where the "too cheap" and "too expensive" curves cross the opposing questions, and an optimal point where "too cheap" and "too expensive" cross each other directly.

You don't need a research budget to run it, four questions in a form sent to your waitlist or beta list beats a guess from browsing five competitors' pricing pages. Say forty responses come back with medians of $3 too cheap, $6 a bargain, $12 getting expensive and $20 too expensive: the acceptable range sits around $6–$12, with the optimal point nearer $9. That's not the $3–5 most founders would have typed in after a quick look at what else is on the app store, and it came from the people who'll be asked to pay it.

Send the form before you touch Stripe

The order matters. Work out the real alternative you're being compared against, decide what you're charging for, then send four questions to the list you already have before a single line of billing code exists. It costs an afternoon and a Google Form, and it replaces a guess with a number the market already told you it would pay.
