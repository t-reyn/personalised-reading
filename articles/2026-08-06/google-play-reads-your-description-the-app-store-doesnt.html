Apple Never Reads Your App Description — Google Play Ranks You on It

6 August 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

App Store and Play Store search run on almost entirely different inputs, so the keyword strategy that works on one platform can quietly sink you on the other.

Two stores, two algorithms

Ship an app to both stores and it's tempting to treat "app store optimisation" as one skill, learned once and applied twice. It isn't. The two platforms decide what surfaces for a search in almost entirely different ways, and the field most builders get backwards is the description: on Apple's store it plays no role in ranking at all; on Google's, it's the main text the algorithm reads.

What Apple's search indexes

Apple's own developer documentation lists exactly four inputs to text relevance: app title, subtitle, the dedicated Keywords field, and primary category. The paragraph a user reads before tapping Get isn't on that list: it exists to convert someone who already found you, not to help them find you.

The Keywords field is the one most indie builders waste. It's capped at 100 characters, comma-separated with no spaces after commas, and Apple explicitly tells you not to spend characters on plurals of words you've already listed, generic terms like "app" or "game", filler words like "the" or "to", or your own app name (your title already covers that). Strip the waste from a typical fitness-app keyword list and you're often left with room for only ten or twelve real terms, which is why niche, specific terms beat generic popular ones: "app" is worthless, "progressive-overload" is a real slot.

Behaviour then compounds on top of relevance. Downloads, ratings, reviews and other engagement signals decide where you sit once a search surfaces you at all, so an app with the right keywords but no reviews still sinks under a rival with worse keywords and four thousand ratings.

Google Play reads the paragraph you wrote for humans

Play has no separate keyword field. Google's own guidance says ranking draws on "metadata (for example, title, description, category)" matched against what a user typed. The description block is the keyword field itself. Write "track your workouts, log progressive overload, hit new personal bests" as a real sentence and you're doing search optimisation and copywriting in the same paragraph. The comma-stuffed keyword list that works in Apple's field would read as spam to a human, and on Google's signal set, would likely rank worse for it too.

Conversion is a separate lever, and Apple lets you test it

App Store Connect ships a built-in test tool for exactly this problem: run up to three alternate treatments of your icon, screenshots or preview video against the original, for up to 90 days, until the result hits 90% confidence. Apple's own case studies show why guessing is a bad plan. Simply Piano tried an app-preview video as the first screenshot and conversion dropped 3%. Peak Brain Training swapped a robot icon for a brain icon and gained 8%. Neither result was obvious from the design brief; it took a live test against real impressions to see which asset converted better, and neither swap cost more than an afternoon to set up.

Sequence it after the manual push, not before

This is where it connects to the ten users you got by messaging people directly rather than running ads: both stores' rankings run partly on behaviour, and behaviour data means nothing until real users have generated some. Optimise your Keywords field and screenshots before you have a handful of reviews and you're tuning an engine with no fuel in it. The sequence that works in practice is manual outreach first: it earns your first real users and your first honest reviews, and only then does store optimisation have behavioural signal worth compounding against. Even a dozen genuine reviews give the ranking algorithm something to work with; zero reviews give it nothing.

This week

Pull up your live listing on both stores. On iOS, check whether your 100-character Keywords field has any wasted characters: plurals, "app", your own app's name. On Android, read your description out loud; if it sounds like a list of search terms rather than a sentence, rewrite it as one. They are not the same job, and optimising for one the way you'd optimise for the other will cost you rank on whichever store you got wrong.
