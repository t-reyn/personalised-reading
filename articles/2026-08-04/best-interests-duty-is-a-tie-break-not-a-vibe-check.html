🏦 Mortgage Broking

The Best Interests Duty Is a Tie-Break Rule, Not a Vibe Check

4 August 2026 · synthesised from 2 sources

Cert IV teaches best interests duty as an ethical standard. The actual law is a narrow conflict-priority rule that only activates when a broker's pay and a client's outcome genuinely point different ways, and the 2020 commission reforms exist to stop that fight from starting in the first place.

Cert IV frames the best interests duty as the profession's ethical spine: always act in the client's interests. The provision that actually enforces it is narrower and more useful than that, and knowing the difference is the first thing that separates a broker who complies from one who's just being pleasant. Section 158LA of the National Consumer Credit Protection Act requires a licensee to act in the consumer's best interests when giving credit assistance. The section doing the real work sits one clause over.

The rule is a tie-break

Section 158LB is a conflict priority rule: where the broker's interests and the client's interests genuinely conflict, the client's interests must be given priority. Not weighed. Not considered alongside other factors. Given priority. That's a tie-break, not a running moral audit of every recommendation you make. It only has work to do when there's an actual tie to break. Most of a broker's day involves no conflict at all: the product that pays the standard commission and the product that suits the client are the same product. The duty isn't asking you to treat that as a test you keep passing. It's asking you to notice the specific moments where it stops being true.

Where the tie actually happens

The clearest one in an ongoing practice is trail commission. Once a loan settles, trail keeps paying for as long as it stays with that lender; refinance the client somewhere cheaper and that income stops. A broker carrying a back book of trail has a standing financial reason to leave a client's loan alone even after a materially better rate turns up elsewhere. That's not a hypothetical edge case: it's the exact shape of conflict s158LB was written to catch, because it's structural, not a one-off bad decision. ASIC's guidance on the duty (RG 273, published June 2020) sets out what resolving it properly looks like: establish the client's actual requirements, assess a genuinely reasonable range of products from your panel against them, and be able to show in the file that the product you recommended met those requirements, not merely that it cleared a servicing calculator.

Redesigning the incentive, not just the outcome

Regulators didn't stop at the conflict rule. The same 2020 reforms rewired the commission structure itself, banning campaign and volume-based payments and standardising most of the industry to a flat upfront-plus-trail model. That's worth sitting with, because it answers a question the duty alone doesn't: if s158LB already forces a broker to favour the client whenever a conflict shows up, why bother touching remuneration at all? Because a rule that only activates once a conflict exists still depends on someone noticing it and enforcing it, file by file. Redesign the incentive instead, and a lot of conflicts never get the chance to occur. There's no live financial reason to favour one lender on volume grounds if every lender pays the same way. The conflict rule is a backstop for what a better-built system still lets through, not the primary fix.

The pull the other way

What gets through is real, and it doesn't only pull toward inertia. Upfront commission is typically repayable to the lender if a loan is discharged within roughly its first year or two on the books, so moving a fresh client too early costs the broker money on the deal just settled. That's a second conflict, and it pulls in the opposite direction from trail: pressure to keep a brand-new client parked rather than move them, even briefly, toward churn. Trail rewards leaving an old loan alone; clawback punishes disturbing a new one. Best interests duty doesn't care which direction the incentive happens to be pointing that day — same test, same answer, client's interest wins either way.

What the file needs to say

The practical shift for a new broker isn't "be more ethical." It's building the habit, at the point you make a recommendation, of writing down why the product you picked served this client's stated requirements, even when it wasn't the cheapest, fastest, or best-paying option on your panel. That note is what safe harbour is actually asking for, and it's cheap to write at the time and expensive to reconstruct later. Keep the panel wide enough that "a reasonable range of products" isn't trivially satisfied by the one lender you already know well, and the moments s158LB governs stop being a compliance risk and start being routine.
