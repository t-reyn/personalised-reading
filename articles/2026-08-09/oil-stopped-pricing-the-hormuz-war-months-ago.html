Oil Stopped Pricing the Hormuz War Months Ago

9 August 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

Brent spiked 65% to US$126 a barrel when Iran shut the Strait of Hormuz in March; six months and fresh tanker strikes later, oil sits near US$78 — because a Saudi pipeline quietly did what diplomacy couldn't.

Three more tankers were hit in the Strait of Hormuz this week, Abu Dhabi's national oil company said — part of 15 attacks on its own fleet alone since the war began on 28 February, which have killed one seafarer and injured 20 more. Brent crude, the benchmark that spiked 65% to US$126 a barrel within the war's first month, barely registered. This morning's live board (6:18am AEST) has WTI at US$78.18, up just 1.2%, a whisker above where Brent started before the first strike landed. Six months into a shooting war over the world's biggest oil chokepoint, the market has largely stopped pricing it as one.

None of this is settled. A framework deal for Iran and Oman to jointly run the strait, with no tolls and free passage for shipping, is reportedly awaiting sign-off from Iran's Supreme National Security Council; a diplomat close to the talks expects approval "shortly," though US backing remains uncertain. Attacks kept landing while that vote sat unresolved. What's changed since March isn't the danger. It's how much of the world's oil supply that danger can still threaten.

Why the same war moves oil less

In March, closing Hormuz meant threatening roughly 15 million barrels a day, the strait's entire pre-war throughput, and Brent repriced accordingly in its steepest monthly jump on record. By 28 March, Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline — a 1,000km line built after the Iran-Iraq war for precisely this scenario — was running at full capacity: 7 million barrels a day, hauled overland to the Red Sea port of Yanbu instead of through the strait. Fortune's reporting on the pipeline put it plainly: the bypass "is one reason oil prices haven't reached the crisis-level highs of previous supply shocks."

That's marginal-barrel pricing. A market doesn't price the total volume under threat; it prices the barrel that clears supply against demand at the edge. When close to half the strait's pre-war flow can now route around it, the barrel Iran can still hold hostage is smaller than it was in March, so the same intensity of attack buys a smaller price move. WTI was still near US$95 in late June; it's US$78 now, drifting lower through fresh strikes rather than jumping on them. You already know the shape of a transitory shock from the ceasefire that crashed oil 8% in July and unwound just as fast. This pipeline is the opposite: it doesn't un-build itself when a truce breaks, so the price it bought stays bought.

Gold is telling a different story

If oil has stopped pricing the war, something else hasn't. Gold is up 3.7% to US$4,399.70 an ounce this morning, the sharpest move on the board by a wide margin, while the VIX fell 1.65% overnight. That split is safe-haven demand at work: gold isn't tied to whether a specific tanker clears a specific strait, it moves on broad uncertainty and store-of-value buying that doesn't care which pipeline is running. Real yields still explain a good share of gold's slower, multi-month climb, but a 3.7% single-session jump against an otherwise calm board reads as investors hedging general Middle East risk while leaving the oil trade to people who track pipeline capacity for a living.

What moves the number from here

None of this makes the war smaller for the people on those tankers, and it doesn't guarantee calm bowser prices: Australian pump prices track import parity off Singapore benchmarks that still move with Brent and Dubai crude, just with less amplitude than a bypass-free Hormuz would allow. The lever that matters from here isn't another attack count. It's whether Iran's Supreme National Security Council ratifies the Oman framework, or lets it collapse the way June's memorandum did in July. Ratification caps the downside further; a collapse won't get oil back to US$126, because the bypass is now absorbing too much of the risk for a repeat of March, but it's the one switch left that could put a real premium back in the price.
