Serviceability Runs on Three Numbers Your Client Has Never Seen

27 July 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

A serviceability assessment doesn't test what a client can afford — it tests a modelled expense floor, a rate buffer nobody pays, and a debt ceiling most borrowers don't know exists. Here's how the three combine, and where each one quietly kills a deal you thought was solid.

A client earning $175,000 a year, no other debts, sitting on six months of genuine savings, can still get knocked back for a $650,000 loan he could comfortably repay at today's rate. Not because a credit assessor doubts him. Because three numbers, none of them his real budget, failed a test before a human ever looked at the file. Cert IV teaches the five Cs: character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions. What actually decides capacity is three specific figures a computer runs before anyone reads the loan purpose.

A budget you didn't write

The first is the Household Expenditure Measure. If a client declares $2,200 a month in living costs and the bank's benchmark for that income band, postcode and household size says $3,100, the system uses $3,100. Not because the client is lying, but because HEM exists precisely so a lender never has to trust a self-reported number the client has every incentive to understate. It's a modelled floor, segmented by income and family size, and until recently it was run by illion. illion is now part of Experian, following the acquisition that folded one of Australia's oldest credit-data houses into a much larger group: same benchmark, different owner, still sitting underneath every servicing calculator on your panel. Two clients with identical declared expenses can get meaningfully different borrowing power once postcode and household size run through the model. That's HEM doing its job, not a lender error worth disputing.

A rate nobody will pay

The second is the buffer. APRA's guidance to lenders, APG 223, sets the expectation that an ADI test a borrower's capacity to repay at a rate well above what's on offer. Industry practice has settled on adding a minimum 3 percentage points to the loan's real rate, or applying a floor rate, whichever is higher. It's why a 0.5-point rate cut from the RBA rarely buys a client the borrowing power a straight-line calculation implies: the test rate moves down with it, not to zero. A client fixated on the advertised rate is asking the wrong question. The number that determines what they can borrow is the rate plus the buffer, and that number barely moves when headlines say rates fell.

A ceiling most clients don't know exists

The third is debt-to-income. A client can clear HEM and the buffer with room to spare and still get extra scrutiny, a lower LVR, or a decline, because the total lending against their income crosses a threshold most panel lenders treat as a red flag on its own, commonly around six times gross income. It isn't an affordability test; a borrower well inside it can still fail on HEM or the buffer, and a borrower who clears both can still get knocked back on DTI alone if a fourth investment property pushes total debt over the line. It functions as a portfolio guardrail as much as an individual one: APRA watches DTI above six across the industry, and a lender close to its own internal limit will tighten before any single file forces the issue.

Run the numbers before the calculator does

None of this changes the legal obligation underneath it. ASIC's responsible lending guidance still requires reasonable inquiries into a client's actual situation and a genuine assessment that a loan isn't unsuitable, not just a servicing calculator pass. But a broker who treats HEM, the buffer and DTI as three separate, checkable numbers can pre-empt a decline instead of explaining one after the event: run the client's real postcode and household size through HEM before quoting borrowing power, check the buffer against the file's actual rate rather than the advertised one, and total existing plus proposed debt against income before a fourth property goes anywhere near a lender. The five Cs are still the framework you talk through with a client. These three numbers are what a machine checks first.
