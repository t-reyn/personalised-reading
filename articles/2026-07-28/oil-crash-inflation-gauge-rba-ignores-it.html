Oil Just Crashed 8% — the RBA's Inflation Gauge Is Built to Ignore It

28 July 2026 · synthesised from 2 sources

WTI dropped 8.3% and Brent fell below US$90 after a US-Iran ceasefire, but the RBA's trimmed mean measure exists precisely to filter out this kind of shock — so don't expect cheaper petrol to buy the next rate cut.

Overnight, West Texas Intermediate crude fell 8.3% to US$81.88 a barrel and Brent dropped below US$90, after Iran reportedly agreed to suspend attacks on US assets provided Washington's own pause in strikes holds, per CNBC's report. It's the sharpest single-day move oil has made in months, and it lands on the same morning traders are pricing incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh to be pressed on the shock at this week's press conference, per CNBC. None of that changes when the RBA moves next — and the reason says more about how inflation actually gets measured than anything in the headline.

This morning's board: the ASX 200 at 8,894.0 (+0.6%), the S&P 500 at 7,413.2 (+0.1%), AUD/USD at 0.6992 (+0.4%), gold barely moved at US$4,077.6/oz (+0.2%). Against all of that, oil's 8.3% fall stands alone — equities higher, gold essentially flat, nothing else on the board close to that scale of move. That's a market reading Middle East de-escalation as a clean net positive rather than a broader flight from risk. It's also the exact shape of shock that rarely reaches the number the RBA is actually watching.

The number that moves, and the one that doesn't

You already know the RBA targets trimmed mean, not headline CPI. What's worth going deeper on is why: headline counts every item in the basket at its full weight, while trimmed mean strips out whichever items moved hardest in either direction that quarter — and fuel routinely tops that list, because it swings on exactly this kind of event. An 8% overnight crash in oil, even if it held for a full quarter, would show up loudly in headline CPI's petrol component and largely vanish from trimmed mean, because trimmed mean's whole design is to exclude the extreme movers rather than average them in. That's not the RBA missing the signal. It's the RBA declining to treat a reversible geopolitical shock as the kind of persistent price pressure the target measure exists to track.

Why "transitory" is doing real work here

Call this a transitory supply shock: a price move driven by an event — a ceasefire, a refinery outage, a shipping-lane scare — that can unwind as fast as it arrived, rather than by a durable shift in supply or demand. The distinction is the difference between a shock that should move policy and one that shouldn't. A central bank that reacted to every transitory shock would be cutting and hiking on the geopolitical news cycle instead of the underlying trend, and would end up adding volatility rather than damping it. Trimmed mean is the mechanical version of that judgement call: it doesn't ask in real time whether a price move looks temporary, it just structurally down-weights whatever moved most, which happens to catch most transitory shocks without the RBA ever having to make the call explicitly. The catch is that the same mechanism can also trim out a genuine, persistent price pressure if it happens to be concentrated in one or two items — a good filter, not a perfect one.

The Warsh test, and what to actually watch

The nearer-term tell isn't tomorrow's ASX open — it's whether the ceasefire holds. Iran's undertaking is explicitly conditional on the US pause continuing, which means an 8% fall built on a truce less than a day old could unwind just as fast. If it does, today's move was noise twice over: once on the way down, once on the reversal, and trimmed mean will have quietly filtered out both. The firmer date to watch is the Fed's, not the RBA's: Kalshi traders are already pricing new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh to be asked directly about the shock at this week's press conference, and whether he frames it as noise or as a genuine input to the US outlook will move the US rate path enough to show up in AUD/USD well before it shows up in any Australian data. That's the number worth checking after Wednesday's press conference — not the price of a barrel of oil that could be back above US$90 by the time you read this.
