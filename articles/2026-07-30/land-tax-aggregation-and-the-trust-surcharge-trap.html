Land Tax Adds Up Your Whole Portfolio — A Trust Gets No Threshold At All

30 July 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

Land tax isn't assessed property by property — it's the combined site value of everything you hold in a state, and in NSW a discretionary trust loses the $1,075,000 tax-free threshold altogether, turning the vehicle you chose for negative gearing into a much bigger annual bill.

A land tax bill doesn't care whether your second investment property sits in your own name or a trust. It cares about the combined value of everything you own in that state, and the vehicle you picked to hold it can turn a modest bill into one worth many multiples more. Buy an investment property in NSW and the state doesn't ask whether it's your first, second or fifth. It adds the site value of every taxable property you hold there into one number, checks that number against a single threshold, and taxes whatever sits above it. Nothing about the mortgage, the rent, or how the loan was structured changes that.

One threshold for the whole portfolio, not one per address

Say you own your home (exempt as a principal place of residence) plus two investment properties in your own name, with land values of $600,000 and $550,000. Neither figure looks alarming alone: both sit comfortably under NSW's general land tax threshold of $1,075,000, frozen at that level since the 2024–25 State Budget stopped indexing it further. But land tax doesn't test them one at a time. Revenue NSW combines the two into a single taxable value of $1,150,000, then applies the threshold: $100 plus 1.6% of the $75,000 above it, a bill of roughly $1,300 a year that didn't exist when you owned one property and does now that you own two. Add a third and the combined figure keeps climbing. The threshold is a one-time gate across your whole NSW footprint, not a fresh allowance for every purchase.

The structure that erases the gate completely

Here's the sharper problem. If those same two properties sit inside a discretionary trust instead, a structure often chosen for the flexibility to stream rental income and losses to different beneficiaries, NSW treats the land differently again: a special or discretionary trust gets no tax-free threshold at all. The $1,075,000 buffer that softened the bill above simply isn't there; the full $1,150,000 is taxed from the first dollar, at roughly 1.6%, for an annual bill closer to $18,000 rather than $1,300. Victoria runs the same aggregation logic at a far smaller scale: its general threshold is just $50,000, and land held through a trust there attracts a surcharge starting at $25,000, half the individual threshold. Every state sets its own numbers, but the shape repeats: trusts pay sooner and more than individuals do, on identical land.

Why this catches people who structured correctly for everything else

None of this makes a trust the wrong choice. The reasons you'd use one are real: protecting an asset from a beneficiary's creditors, splitting rental income where it's taxed lightest, keeping a future inheritance out of a family law dispute. None of them disappear because land tax costs more inside a trust. The failure is choosing the entity for those reasons and never separately pricing the land tax that comes with it, so a structure that made perfect sense for succession planning lands an unbudgeted five-figure annual cost the moment it holds a second property.

Model the land tax before you model the loan

Before your next purchase, run the combined land value through your state revenue office's calculator under the entity you're planning to use, not your current one. An identical property can cost four figures a year in one name and five in another. If you already hold property through a trust, get your accountant to check today's combined land value against the surcharge threshold now, rather than finding out on next year's assessment notice.
