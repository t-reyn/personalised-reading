The Genetic Testing Ban Trades a Capped Risk for an Uncapped One

29 July 2026 · synthesised from 3 sources

The Institute's submission on the government's total genetic-testing ban backs it, rightly — but its own request for population-level monitoring data is the tell that a sum-insured-capped anti-selection risk just became uncapped, with nothing yet priced to replace the cap.

The Actuaries Institute's submission on the government's genetic testing ban runs to two pages, agrees with almost everything in the draft, and closes with one request that does the real work: it asks Treasury for a mechanism to "regularly collect and monitor population-level genetic testing data." Read as a compliance nicety, that's housekeeping. Read as a pricing actuary, it's an admission that the ban just removed a control nobody has replaced with anything yet.

The exposure draft of the Insurance Contracts Amendment (Genetic Testing Protections in Life Insurance) Regulations 2026, now with Treasury, implements a ban Parliament has already legislated: an insurer can't use, or even ask about, an adverse predictive genetic test result in underwriting or claims assessment, for any listed condition, at any sum insured. No ceiling, no carve-out for large policies. The question worth arguing isn't whether the ban is fair to the person who took the test. It is. It's whether a rule with no sum-insured limit reopens, exactly where it costs the most, an anti-selection channel the industry spent years trying to cap rather than close.

The steelman: the old cap was never defensible

A competent actuary could hold the opposite view, and for years the evidence backed them. The Financial Services Council's 2019 moratorium let insurers still ask about and act on a genetic test result once a policy's sum insured cleared a threshold. That moratorium was voluntary, industry-set, and partial by design. The 2018 Parliamentary Joint Committee on Corporations and Financial Services had already called that arrangement insufficient and recommended an urgent ban. The Australian Genetics and Life Insurance Moratorium Monitoring project spent three years checking whether the moratorium actually worked and reported in 2023 that it didn't: people kept avoiding genetic testing, including tests with real preventive value, because any result above the threshold could still cost them cover. Concede that plainly: the threshold was arbitrary, it deterred exactly the testing behaviour a health system wants to encourage, and the evidence said so well before this year's legislation caught up. On the fairness ledger, the ban is a clean improvement and the Institute is right to back it.

Why the cap existed in the first place

None of that touches the reason a cap sat there at all. Informed non-disclosure only pays off financially once the cover on offer is large enough to matter, which is why anti-selection risk was never going to scale in a straight line with sum insured. Most group life exposure never faced this problem to begin with: default cover inside superannuation is issued automatically up to an automatic acceptance limit, no health questions asked of anyone, so a ban on using genetic information changes nothing there: there was nothing to disclose in the first place. The risk sits precisely where an insurer already asks health questions and prices on the answers: voluntary top-up cover above a fund's automatic acceptance limit, and fully underwritten retail term life. That's where someone who knows their own risk, and who previously would have been capped or referred for further scrutiny above a threshold, can now simply buy as much as an insurer will issue.

Sizing the freed cohort

Model it, not against the actual historical thresholds (they varied by product and insurer, and none of the source documents here state one reliably enough to quote), but against a clean illustrative structure that captures the shape of the change. Take a retail term book of 100,000 lives, average sum insured $750,000, priced to a combined mortality-and-TPD incidence of 0.15% a year: baseline expected claims of $112.5 million annually. Suppose, illustratively, that 1 in 500 people carry a highly penetrant hereditary condition on the protected list, a combined order of magnitude across conditions like Huntington's disease, hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndromes and familial hypercholesterolaemia, not a specific published figure, putting 200 such people in this book. Under the old capped regime, say each was limited to $500,000 without further scrutiny. Under the ban, nothing stops them buying to $1.5 million, and their true incidence for the insured event runs at an illustrative 4 times the book's baseline rate.

The newly uninsured-for exposure is $1 million per person across 200 people: $200 million of sum insured that wasn't priced for this risk before. At an elevated incidence of 0.6% (4 times 0.15%), that's $1.2 million a year in additional expected claims this book wasn't carrying under the old cap. Restate that number three ways: an absolute $1.2 million a year; spread across the whole 100,000-life book, about $12 a policyholder a year, near enough 23 cents a week added to everyone's risk premium to fund it; against the $112.5 million baseline claims cost, a 1.1% uplift. Small against the whole book. Not small against a reinsurance treaty priced before anyone modelled it, and not zero, which is what every current pricing basis assumes.

The data the ban makes unreadable

Here's the part an experience study will never catch, and it's the strongest argument in this piece. Before the ban, a file review after a claim could at least establish whether a genetic result had been withheld above a threshold. After it, genetic status isn't collected, isn't disclosed and isn't recorded anywhere an insurer can see, by design: that's the entire point of the ban. So the shift this piece has just modelled is, going forward, structurally invisible to the pricing team meant to detect it: not a gap at the edge of the data, a population the data was never built to observe. That's exactly why the Institute's own ask, for population-level rather than individual-level genetic testing data, is the only instrument left standing. It can't tell an appointed actuary whether any one policyholder is anti-selecting. It's the only way anyone will ever know whether the cohort this modelling describes exists in the real numbers, or was smaller than feared.

What to do before the regulations are finalised

This doesn't argue for reopening the ban. The fairness case is settled and shouldn't be relitigated. It argues for treating voluntary top-up and retail large-sum-insured bands as a distinct, wide-margin segment starting now, in the same review cycle as the next reinsurance treaty renewal, rather than folding them into an experience study that can no longer see the risk it's meant to price. And it argues for using the current consultation window to push the Institute's monitoring request from a polite closing line into a condition attached to commencement, not an afterthought raised after the data problem has already made itself unfixable.
